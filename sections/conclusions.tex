\section{Conclusions and open problems}

We have provided a series of approximation algorithms for solving decision tree and set covering problems subject to precedence constraints. Our main contribution is a series of reductions that enable us to systematically relate the two types of problems independently of the specific structure of the precedence constraints. As an immediate corollary, we obtain general $\tilde{\bigo}\br{m^{1/3}}$-approximation algorithms for both types of problems as well as case-specific polylogarithmic approximations for both inforest and outforest precedence constraints. These two classes are natural to study, since trees are among the most prevalent structures encountered in practice.

Prior to this work, almost no non-trivial approximation algorithms were known for either of these problems, even for the simplest of our variants. Among possible explanations for this state of affairs is the fact that achieving our results demanded developing an elaborate and systematic reduction \DDfix{framework and using non-greedy} procedures. This is especially evident in the average-case \DDfix{criterion: the algorithm for  $\ProblemPCMSSC$ relied on an elaborate procedure that used a budgeted covering algorithm as a subroutine but was itself not greedy.} This raises two questions. First, is it possible to show that an algorithm that always picks a test (with its closure) maximizing some kind of information gain also achieves a good approximation ratio for either $\ProblemPCWCDT$ or $\ProblemPCACDT$? Second, is it possible to simplify the procedure for $\ProblemPCACDT$ along with its analysis while maintaining a good approximation ratio?

We have complemented our algorithmic results with a series of conditional lower bounds, providing inapproximability results for most of our variants including both outforest and general precedence constraints. We find it particularly surprising that approximating the decision tree problem generalizes the task of planted dense subgraph detection in a random graph. This result, although quite unexpected, extends the lengthy line of work on the connection between average-case complexity and inapproximability of problems (to read more about such connections, see \cite{Feige2002RelationsBetweenAverageCaseComplexityAndApproximationComplexity}). Whether or not polynomial-factor inapproximability results based on P vs. NP can be established for our problem remains an open question.

In this work, we have studied average-case decision tree construction under the assumption that the probability distribution over all possible hypotheses is uniform. In particular, extending our results to non-uniform distributions would require new techniques. It seems to us that our analysis relies heavily on the uniformity assumption. However, it is possible that some of the techniques we have developed can be adapted to handle non-uniform distributions as well. For example, our algorithms for covering problems should be easily adaptable to include non-uniform weights on the elements of the universe, which might be useful for encoding non-uniform distributions over the hypotheses. However, it is not clear to us whether we can adapt the reduction from $\ProblemPCACDT$ to $\ProblemPCMSSC$ to work for that setup as well. For uniform probabilities, Corollary~\ref{cor:PCACDT-outforest-hardness} establishes $\bigo(\log^{1-\epsilon} n)$-hardness with outforest precedence constraints. For non-uniform probabilities, we expect that a stronger $\bigo(\log^{2-\epsilon} n)$ bound could be obtained under the same complexity assumption by adapting the reduction in Theorem~\ref{thm:PCMSSC-outforest-hardness}. One possible approach is to create a low-probability hypothesis for each group and a high-probability hypothesis in place of the dummy elements, then replace sets with tests. This argument is only a sketch and remains to be established.

Another direction for future research is to enrich the \textsc{Optimal Decision Tree} with more types of constraints borrowed from scheduling theory. For example, one may consider release dates on the tests or due dates on the elements. In such a setup it is also possible to optimize for different cost functions, such as the maximum lateness or the total tardiness. The same types of generalizations are also applicable to the set covering problems. To the best of our knowledge, no results of this nature are known for any of these problems.
